## Research Overview

This study interrogates the crisis of social cohesion and cultural conflict across
21st-century Europe. The research establishes a theoretical intersection by
synthesizing three distinct analytical frameworks: the policy failures of
European multiculturalism, the resurgence of Ibn Khaldun's concept of *asabiyyah*
(group solidarity), and Samuel P. Huntington's "Clash of Civilizations" thesis.
The article asserts that in a post-9/11 and post-industrial landscape, primordial
attachments — such as religion, language, and ancestral identity — have
superseded traditional political ideologies as the primary drivers of both
localized conflict and global statecraft.

## Theoretical Triad

- **The Multicultural Crisis:** Analyzes how economic recessions, resource
  competition, and demographic shifts have fractured European integration
  frameworks (from French assimilationism to British multiculturalism), leading
  to systemic social exclusion and political polarization.
- **The Resurgence of Asabiyyah:** Adapts Ibn Khaldun's 14th-century sociological
  model to modern state dynamics, arguing that contemporary nationalism,
  localism, and right-wing populism represent a protective revival of
  *asabiyyah* — a reactionary group solidarity deployed against perceived
  cultural dilution and globalization.
- **Civilizational Fault Lines:** Integrates Huntington's framework to
  demonstrate how internal social fragmentation within European nation-states
  mirrors broader, macro-level civilizational friction between "Core States" and
  "kin countries."

## Key Findings & Contemporary Impact

- **Validation of Civilizational Divides:** The research proves that the
  perceived collapse of European multiculturalism operates as a localized stress
  test, validating civilizational fault-line theories in everyday sociopolitical
  life.
- **Modernization vs. Westernization:** The study highlights a growing global
  decoupling where non-Western societies and migrant communities achieve
  technological and economic modernization without adopting Western secular
  liberalism.
- **Digital Asabiyyah & Virtual Polarization:** Explores how digital media
  simultaneously constructs a global youth subculture while accelerating virtual
  polarization, creating new online "fault lines" that mirror real-world
  geopolitical tensions.

## Conclusion & Policy Implications

The article warns that as the international order transitions into an era of
deglobalization and shrinking resources, the friction between declining
multiculturalism, hyper-nationalist *asabiyyah*, and civilizational divides risks
devolving into a zero-sum conflict. The study concludes that sustainable global
stability requires moving away from zero-sum integration models toward
intentional, reality-based intercultural dialogue and structural inclusivity.
